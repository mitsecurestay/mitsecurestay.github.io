Finding a Furnished Room in Washington, DC

For traveling nurses, contractors, international visitors, relocating professionals and anyone who needs a place to live in DC without buying furniture or setting up utilities.

By MIT Secure Stay · Last reviewed November 2026

The short answer: a furnished room in DC can be a bedroom in a shared house, a private suite inside a home, or a furnished studio. Before you rent one, confirm exactly what furniture and which utilities are included, whether the kitchen, bathroom and laundry are shared, the lease length and notice period, and what you pay and when. Don't pay anything until you've seen the space, in person or by video, and signed a lease.

What types of furnished housing are there in DC?

"Furnished" covers several quite different setups. Knowing which one you're looking at tells you most of what to expect on privacy, cost and lease terms.

What furnishings should I confirm?

Listings often say "fully furnished" without saying what that means. Ask for a list, or photos of the actual room, and check:

  • Bed: the size (twin, full, queen), and whether a mattress comes with it. Ask whether bedding and towels are provided or whether you bring your own.
  • Work space: a desk and chair, if you'll do any charting, studying or remote work from your room.
  • Storage: closet or dresser space, and whether you'll have room in the kitchen and fridge.
  • Kitchen: whether cookware, dishes and appliances are there for you to use.
  • Laundry: in the home, in the building or off-site, and whether it costs extra.
  • Extras: a TV, blackout curtains, a lock on the bedroom door, parking.

Which utilities should be included?

"Utilities included" is one of the most useful things a furnished rental can offer, especially if you're only in the city for a few months. It's also one of the most common places for surprises. Get the answer in writing, and confirm each of these:

  • Electricity and gas
  • Water, sewer and trash
  • Internet, and whether it's fast enough for video calls
  • Whether there's a cap, a surcharge for heavy use, or a separate bill for anything

DC summers are hot and humid and winters can be cold, so heating and cooling are worth asking about directly. Find out how the temperature is set in a shared home.

Shared housing or a private living space: which fits you?

A room in a shared home tends to suit you if…

  • you want the simplest, most cost-conscious way to live in the city;
  • you don't mind sharing a kitchen and living areas with a few other adults;
  • you like having other people around in a city that's new to you.

A private suite tends to suit you if…

  • you work nights or rotating shifts and need to sleep at odd hours;
  • a private bathroom matters a lot to you;
  • you want more separation while still having furniture and utilities taken care of.

Whichever you choose, ask how many people live in the home and what the household expectations are: guests, quiet hours, cleaning and shared supplies. A good host will tell you before you apply.

Questions to ask before you rent

  1. Who is the landlord? Am I renting from the owner, a property manager, or another tenant?
  2. How many people live in the home, and which spaces are shared?
  3. Exactly what furniture and which utilities are included?
  4. How long is the lease, and how much notice do I need to give to leave?
  5. What's the deposit, when is it due, and what is it applied to?
  6. How do I pay rent, and which payment methods do you accept?
  7. What are the house rules on guests, quiet hours and shared areas?
  8. How are maintenance requests handled, and how much notice do I get before someone enters my room?
  9. Can I see the room by live video if I'm not in DC yet?
  10. What does the application involve, and how long does it usually take?

What should I understand before signing a lease?

  • Lease length and notice. Furnished rooms aren't automatically short-term or month-to-month. Read the term and the notice period. If your assignment could change, ask how an early move-out is handled before you sign, not after.
  • DC or Maryland. Many "DC-area" rooms are actually in the Maryland suburbs, and DC and Maryland each have their own landlord-tenant rules. Check which one your home is in, because it affects your lease.
  • Money and timing. Know the deposit amount, when the first month's rent is due, and whether any other fees apply.
  • House rules. In shared housing the house rules matter as much as the lease. Ask to read them before you commit.

How do I avoid rental scams?

Scammers target people relocating from out of town because they can't easily visit in person. Be cautious if someone:

  • asks for a deposit or "holding fee" before you've seen the space and signed a lease;
  • won't show the room, in person or by live video;
  • wants payment by gift card or wire transfer, or pushes you to decide immediately;
  • offers a price far below other similar listings.

How do I plan my commute in DC?

DC is divided into four quadrants (NW, NE, SE and SW), and the region is served by Metrorail and Metrobus, plus regional commuter rail and local bus systems. Distance on a map can be misleading. Two homes the same number of miles from your hospital or office can have very different commutes.

  • Check the trip door to door in a maps app, at the time you'll actually travel. For shift workers, that can mean an early-morning or late-night trip.
  • Compare transit and driving. If you'll drive, ask about parking at the home and at work.
  • Walk the last stretch on street view, so you know what the route from the station or stop is like.
